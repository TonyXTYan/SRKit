Relativistic Navigator

Every star map is made of old light: each star is drawn where it was when the light now reaching you left it. Here you ride a rocket that accelerates steadily (1 g feels like home gravity) to any star or galaxy, and watch what changes as your speed approaches light's: how much time passes for you and for everyone else, how the sky crowds ahead of you, how fast other places' history plays, and why "now" far away has no single answer. Plan a ride (flip-and-burn: accelerate half way, turn round, brake) or fly a circular orbit at constant speed. Compare what a station at rest in the galaxy would measure with what you measure aboard, and look around the full 360° sky. Every number is computed live from the formulas of special relativity. 9,549 real stars, plus 20,000 to 600,000 points that fill the Milky Way and 20,000 for Andromeda at realistic density; any of them can be a destination.

Maps

Reach from here (green): where a go-flip-burn at the current drive (burn on a fixed heading, flip, brake) can leave you at rest, n ship-years from now, starting from where you are and how fast you are moving. The × is the soonest you can be at rest at all: brake now. The solid green line is your plan's own isochrone. The maps are seen from above, so every contour is drawn in the galactic plane through you: it passes through stars in that plane, and through the destination when the destination is in it. A destination far above or below the plane sits inside its isochrone on the map. At rest the contours are the ship-years rings; along your line of flight they are the fastest possible; off it, a turning burn could do a little better, so there they are conservative. Each contour is a set of places, drawn through the same projection as the stars, so it passes through the same stars in every frame. Spread on screen picks the budgets so the contours cross the rays ahead, abeam and astern of you at even spacing out to the map's edge, so there are contours behind you at every stage of the ride (how many follows the Rings setting); Round ship-years uses round values instead.

Spacetime · 2+1D (classic)

Galactic plane across, the chosen frame's time up, light at 45°. Drag to turn, wheel to zoom; hover a dot for details, tap it to view from that worldline. Each dot is where your past light cone (cyan) cuts a worldline: the sky you actually see. Those events, and the cone, are the same in every frame, so the dots stay put on the cone when you switch. Each frame's "now" plane (hollow rings) is a coordinate convention that tilts from frame to frame; nothing on it is observable: what anyone can actually know is what has reached them, which is why the map shows only "seen". Home, the destination and the Galactic centre are drawn bold in their label colours (switch off with "Highlight"). The optional test travellers are imaginary observers launched from the start of the ride at 0.5c, 0.9c and 0.99c. Height above or below the galactic plane is dropped.

per grid step

Across. "Distance from you" (the default) keeps each chart's own distance from you, whatever the direction: your clock (galactic time in the galactic frame) runs up the left edge, every body sits exactly where that chart puts it, and everything you see now lies on one 45° line down from you, your past light cone. "Along an axis" keeps a signed position along a line through you toward the destination (your line of flight), home, the Galactic centre, a landmark or any body you click, and projects everything onto it, fading with distance from it; the Maps panel draws the axis. Along your line of flight, bodies on the line are exact and the grids are single lines. On a circular orbit there is no line of flight: every body is still placed exactly, the grids and the other frames' "now"s are drawn along your heading now (ahead, and dotted astern), and the axis can point at the star you orbit or any other body. Up: galactic time in the galactic frame, your own clock in the other three. Your light cones (cyan: the light reaching you now; amber: the light you send now) are 45° lines through you in every chart. Two grids show how the charts differ: the galactic grid (blue-grey, dashed), beacons at rest one step apart and lines of galactic time; your radar grid (amber), lines of constant radar time and radar distance. Each is straight in its own chart and bends in the others, most while you burn. Once you move, another frame's grid line depends on direction, so in "distance from you" it is drawn toward the destination and, dotted, toward home. The flashes are pulses sent by home (cyan) and the destination (violet) every grid step of galactic time: their spacing when they reach you is the Doppler shift. Solid: what you have seen. Dashed: not seen yet, drawn from the plan of the ride and stars at rest (a model, not an observation). Hover any line or dot for what it is and its value under the pointer; click a dot to make that body the axis; drag to pan, wheel or pinch to zoom, double-click to fit.

Sky sphere · 360°

Full screen (or full window) sky sphere: drag to look, pinch or wheel to zoom. Phone gyro and VR headsets (WebXR) work when this page is opened as an ordinary web page over HTTPS; some embedded viewers block motion sensors and XR. "Enter VR" goes straight into a headset. A control panel floats below your view (drag its title bar to move it; Panel − and + resize it): move through ship time, play, keep playing, change speed or frame, and turn the sky so that dead ahead is in front of you. Pinch (or pull the trigger) on a star to see what you are seeing of it, and fly there. The spacetime diagram below has its own 3D view and VR model.

The panorama is the whole sky unrolled: dead ahead at the centre, galactic north up. In the 360° view or a VR headset, turning your head only looks around; aberration depends on the ship's velocity, not on where you look. Each filled-galaxy point spreads its light over its real size (about 800 ly near the Sun), so nearby ones blur into the Milky Way band and distant ones stay sharp. Colour is the Doppler-shifted blackbody temperature and brightness includes the D² boost in flux; far ahead both are schematic, because real starlight is shifted out of the visible band altogether.

Knowledge plane

The horizontal axis is how far into home's history you have seen; the vertical axis is the same for the destination (+d/c). Light rays are the grid, staying home is the diagonal, and ship time along any path is √(du·dv). A thrusting ship can never pass c/a on the home axis: that limit is the Rindler horizon.

Orbit notes

    Two frames, same photons

    Picture a relay station at rest in the galaxy, sitting exactly where you are as you pass. It catches the same photons you do. Because it is not moving through the galaxy it sees true directions and true colours, and it dates each star's light by galactic distance: that is the galactic frame, centred on you. Aboard, the ship frame sees the same photons aberrated toward the bow and Doppler-shifted, and it dates each star's light at galactic light age × D. At the flip of a 1 g crossing of the galaxy, home's image is 50,000 years old to the station and 0.48 yr old to you. Every frame agrees on the date stamped by the source's own clock, so the hover cards lead with that.

      Every number on this page is computed live from the closed-form results of special relativity (constant proper acceleration, aberration, Doppler shift, light travel time); nothing is simulated step by step. Star data: the SIMBAD database, operated at CDS, Strasbourg, France (Wenger et al. 2000), under the Open Database License 1.0; star names from the IAU Catalog of Star Names (CC BY). Label priority uses a rough mass estimate from luminosity. Landmark distances are rounded literature values; the small crosses are evenly spaced reference points, not real objects.
